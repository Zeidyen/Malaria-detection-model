# Literature review: satellite and climate covariates, routine-data outbreak detection, and land-cover change for malaria in Ghana

Date: 2026-10-28. Method: targeted web searches (PubMed via E-utilities, PMC, journal pages, DOI resolvers, institutional repositories, arXiv API). This was not a systematic review. Every paper below was confirmed to exist from a retrieved record (PubMed record, journal/repository page, or arXiv entry). Unless marked "full text", the claims come from the **abstract only**. Items marked "unverified" could not be fully confirmed.

Transmission labels: "epidemic-prone" = unstable/seasonal-low transmission where epidemics are the classical concern (highlands, desert fringe, southern margin); "stable" = perennial or seasonal high/moderate endemic transmission (most of Ghana).

Overview of Section A. The evidence that climate/EO adds forecast skill is clearest in epidemic-prone or low-transmission settings (Botswana, Ethiopian and Burundian highlands, southern Zambia, Sahara fringe). In stable settings (Uganda, western Kenya lowlands, Mozambique), climate is used but the abstracts either report mixed site-dependent value (Zinszer 2015) or do not compare against a seasonality/autoregression-only baseline. The only study we found that put weather-based alerts and case-based detection on one cost-benefit scale (prevented cases vs alerts) is Teklehaimanot 2004 [4], in epidemic-prone Ethiopia.

## Section D. Reviews

- **Mategula et al. 2025** [43], PLOS Global Public Health 5(5):e0003751. Scoping review of malaria EWS: 6,233 records, 30 studies from 16 countries; most were time-series forecasts with rainfall and temperature; no standardized evaluation criteria; problems with automation, scalability, and linking predictions to action (abstract/PMC page; full text available at PMC12077768, not read in full).
- **Mabaso & Ndlovu 2012** [44], Public Health 126:909-919. Critical review of climate-driven malaria epidemic literature in sub-Saharan Africa (PubMed 1990-2009, 35 studies): operational climate-based MEWS remained hard because of complex causal pathways and imprecise malaria data (catalogue record and abstract only).
- **Zinszer et al. 2012** [45], BMJ Open 2:e001992. Scoping review of malaria forecasting (29 studies): climate predictors used consistently (rainfall, humidity, temperature, NDVI); accuracy could not be compared across studies because evaluation measures differed; called for common accuracy measures and non-environmental predictors (abstract).
- **Wimberly et al. 2021** [46], Trends in Parasitology 37:525-537. Review of satellite observations for malaria: long records for climate-malaria analysis, high-resolution data for land-use change (agriculture, deforestation, water management), SAR and very-high-resolution imagery, and cloud platforms (abstract).
- **Climate value in stable vs epidemic-prone settings.** We found no systematic review that quantified the added skill of climate covariates over seasonality plus autoregression, stratified by transmission stability. The indirect evidence is: (i) in epidemic-prone Ethiopia, history-only seasonal adjustment beat ARIMA but authors still called for weather predictors [3], and weather-based alerts were within 5% of case-based detection but earlier [4]; (ii) in highly endemic Uganda, clinical predictors were retained in every best model and environmental value was site-dependent [10]; (iii) the scoping reviews [43, 45] report that heterogeneous evaluation metrics prevent cross-study comparison. This is our synthesis of abstracts, not a finding of any single review.

## Section G. Gap statement

Across the literature we retrieved (not a systematic search), satellite and climate covariates have been used mainly for **forecasting**, and their demonstrated value is concentrated in epidemic-prone or low-transmission settings: Botswana [1, 2], the Ethiopian and Burundian highlands [4, 5, 14], southern Zambia [16] and the Saharan fringe of Mali [19]. In stable, high-transmission settings the evidence is thinner and mixed. In Uganda, clinical predictors were retained in every best model and the value of environmental predictors depended on the site [10]. The Mozambique exceedance-probability system [12] and the Ugandan Bayesian model [11] use climate inside the model but, according to their abstracts, do not report detection gains against a seasonality/autoregression-only baseline. We found only one study that put weather-informed alerts and case-based detection on a common prevented-cases-versus-alerts scale [4], and it was set in epidemic-prone Ethiopia with weekly district data. Detection-method comparisons on routine malaria data [35, 36, 38, 39] come from East Africa (Kenya, Ethiopia, Uganda). They judge methods against retrospectively defined "epidemics" rather than injected synthetic outbreaks. None of them adds satellite covariates to the detection model. **We found no study that measures the added detection skill of satellite/reanalysis layers as extra outbreaks detected at a fixed false-alert rate. We also found no national, district-level outbreak detection system validated with injected outbreaks for any stable-transmission West African country, including Ghana.** In Ghana, national district-level DHIMS2 analyses exist for mapping [27], covariate-free forecasting [33] and unsupervised anomaly detection [34]. Climate-malaria studies there are mostly explanatory and use station data at hospital, metro, regional or zonal scale [23-26, 30].

**We found no study that formally classifies malaria alerts as transmission versus reporting/health-system change using total OPD attendance and test positivity.** The data-quality literature does motivate this, from Ghana [55] and elsewhere [53, 54, 56]. It shows that routine counts are affected by completeness, under-reporting and testing practice. One review explicitly notes that missing testing denominators can make changes in testing look like changes in transmission [57]. During the COVID-19 period in Uganda, OPD attendance and positivity moved independently [58]. On land-cover change, mining has been shown to modify climate-malaria relationships at sub-national scale in the Venezuelan Amazon [47]. In Ghana, mining-malaria evidence is limited to community cross-sectional surveys [48, 49] and a scoping review [50]. Galamsey expansion has been mapped from Landsat [51], but **we found no study that links satellite-derived galamsey or land-cover change to district-level malaria time series or to outbreak alerts in Ghana.** These gaps are stated with the caveat that the search was targeted, used mostly abstracts, and may have missed grey literature, non-English work, and studies indexed outside PubMed.
